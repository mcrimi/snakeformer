Where it looks

To write one cell of the new frame the model looks back over everything before it: the old board, your move, and the part of the new frame it has already written. Point at a cell on the left to see what it consulted for that cell in the last finished turn. A cell far from the snake needs only the cell above it in the old board. The interesting ones are the head, the tail and the food.

Layer

Square size is the share of attention, averaged over the heads of a layer; with “All”, the largest value over the layers. Outlines mark the snake and the food. Attention shows what the model read, which is not the same as why it answered as it did.

One picture, two snakes

The model gets one frame and one move. Nothing else. So everything it needs to know must be in the frame. In 2026 I learned this the hard way: without a separate character for the tail, the model could not know which end of the snake to erase, and the snake grew for ever. Marking the tail with # fixed it.

Rebuilding the project, I found the fix was not complete. The body is drawn with one character, O, which does not say which way the snake runs. When the tail touches two body cells, the picture cannot say which of them comes next, and that cell is the tail of the next frame. Below are two different snakes that draw exactly the same picture. Move both one step to the right and the pictures differ.

How often does it happen? Never for a short snake, and more and more as it grows and coils. Over a set of positions weighted towards long snakes, about 3% of ordinary moves have a picture with two readings.

The repair is to draw the body with arrows, each segment pointing to the next. Then one frame holds the whole snake and the question has one answer. The retrained model on this page reads and writes boards that way, which is why its text looks different from the 2026 one.

Does it matter in practice? I expected it to, and it did not. I trained the same small network twice, once with each drawing, and tested both on 1,000 positions that have two readings. They score the same.

A model that truly had to guess between two readings would be right half the time. It does not have to guess. In these games snakes lie in habitual ways, so one reading is far more likely than the other and the model learns which. The flaw is real, and the habits of the player hide it. Arrows are the right drawing; they are not why any model here is better than another.

What I found

In 2026 I judged the model by playing it. It felt right, and I published it. This time I measured it, against the real rules, on 200 whole games (75,840 frames) and on 6,000 positions chosen to be hard: long snakes, meals, collisions. A frame counts as right only if every one of its 256 cells is right.

1. Ninety-eight per cent right is a short game

The published model writes 98.3% of frames exactly right in ordinary play. That sounds like a finished game. It is not, because a game is hundreds of frames and one wrong frame is enough to leave the real game behind. Half of the games had gone wrong by turn 156, and only 10 of 200 were still right after 400 turns.

This is the difference between a model of a game and a game. The rules are right every time. A model that is right 98 times in 100 is a different game after a minute, and in the play above you can watch the moment it happens.

2. A model is good where its data was

The published model is strong in ordinary play and weak away from it: 82% on the hard positions. Asked to turn straight back into its own neck, it gets 19% right. It never had to learn that, because the terminal game refuses the key before the model sees it.

For this page I trained a network a third of the size on positions built to cover the hard cases. It reaches 98% on the hard positions, and then does worse than the published model where it counts, in ordinary play: 95%. My hard positions had too few plain games in them. Neither model learned the rules. Each learned the positions it was shown.

Frames exactly right, by kind of position. The last row is ordinary play; the others are the hard positions, none of them seen in training by the retrained models.

3. Learning from its own mistakes: two ways, one works

The part of the 2026 project I liked most was Shadow Mode: the real rules run beside the model, and when the model slips you press a key and it takes a training step on that one mistake. Mistakes that touch the head, the food or a death count fifty times. The published model had been through some of this by hand. Here I let it run unattended for 300 games, each ending in a death, and measured the model every 30 games on games and positions it was not being corrected on.

It does not settle. Each correction fixes the frame in front of it and moves everything else a little. The published model ends slightly better in play than it started, after dipping well below on the way. The retrained model ends worse on both counts. With a player that never dies the loop teaches the model that nobody dies: frames in play rise to 99.4% while deaths at a wall fall from 97% right to 14%.

The fifty-fold weight turns out to do nothing. The step is cut to a fixed length before it is applied, and every one of the 1,014 steps was long enough to be cut: a typical unweighted mistake was 28 on a scale where the limit is 0.5, a weighted one 3,022. After the cut they are the same size.

The other way is in the 2026 code as well, as the data option I called DAgger: let the model shadow many games, keep every frame it gets wrong, then train on them. I mixed the mistakes half and half with the positions the model first learned from, so that it rehearses what it knew while it fixes what it did not. Three rounds of that took the published model from 98.3% of frames right in play to 99.8%, and from 82% to 99.7% on the hard positions. 124 of its 200 games now run the full 400 turns without one wrong frame; before, 10 did. Part of the gain on hard positions comes from the rehearsal half, since those were positions the published model had never been trained on. Each round found fewer new mistakes: 1,037 in the first, 102 in the third.

The small retrained model gains too, from 95% to 99% in play, but it stays behind, and it still misses most of the rare deaths where the snake boxes itself in. Both are in the chooser at the top. The small one is the default because it writes three times faster; the published one after its mistakes is the closest thing here to the real game.

Before → after, measured on 60 games and 6,000 hard positions that neither method trained on. “One at a time” is Shadow Mode left running; “collect, then retrain” is three rounds of 600 games.

4. How it is trained matters more than how the board is drawn

The 2026 training joined all the examples into one long text, cut windows from it at random places, and scored every character. Half of what it scored is the input board and the move, which the model is never asked to produce, and a window could begin anywhere inside an example. The retraining gives the model one example at a time, always in the same place, and scores only the frame it is asked to write.

Same network, same positions, the same reading per step: after 20,000 steps the 2026 way gets 74% of the hard positions right and 62% of frames in play, and has not learned to eat. The other way gets 98% and 95%. The two drawings of the board, trained the same way, finish together. The published model got as far as it did on the 2026 recipe by being three times larger and training for longer.

5. Can randomness be learned?

In 2026 I gave up on food that appears at random and made its position a fixed function of the head, writing that a model trained to predict the next character cannot learn a random outcome. I tested that. I trained the same network on games with random food and asked it, after each meal, how likely food is at each free cell as it writes the frame.

It learned the odds. Where I went wrong was in reading the answer: I always took the single most likely character, and with many free cells left “no food here” is always the most likely, so food never appeared. If each character is drawn from the model’s own odds instead, 91% of 300 frames are legal, with the right snake and exactly one food, and the food lands evenly over the free cells. The model could do it. The decoding could not.

6. Two corrections to what I published

The README described a model of 4 layers, 8 heads and 0.93 million parameters. The checkpoint on Hugging Face has 6 layers, 6 heads and 2.87M parameters; I must have published a later, larger one and not updated the text. And the file is 163 MB for a model that needs 11: the rest is 36 copies of the attention mask, one per head, a block of ones and zeros saved along with the weights. Stored as 16-bit numbers the model is 5.7 MB, gives the same answers, and is what your browser downloaded.

How it was built

The 2026 project was a terminal game with the model inside it. The rules now live in a small tested module that plays move for move like the terminal game. Around it are two ways to draw a board, a trainer, the two ways of learning from mistakes, and the measurements above, each a command you can run again.

The model on this page is not a recording and there is no server. The network is a few hundred lines of JavaScript, the weights are a file of 16-bit numbers, and each character is computed on your device as you watch. A test feeds the same boards to PyTorch and to the JavaScript and requires the same characters and the same attention.

engine.py ── the rules of Snake, without the terminal
 ├─ encoding.py      a board as text: H O #, or arrows
 ├─ train.py         one example per sequence, or the 2026 stream
 ├─ online.py        Shadow Mode: one mistake, one step
 ├─ experiments.py   the runs; collect mistakes, then retrain
 ├─ analysis.py      experiments.json ──→ charts on this page
 └─ export.py        weights as 16-bit ──→ the model you played
model.js, game.js ── the same network and rules in the browser
